The DoD Deal Did Something Nobody Predicted

2026-03-03 · Field Notes

A 295% surge in ChatGPT uninstalls after news of its DoD deal isn't a statistic I expected to be writing about in a business operations context. But here we are.

Let me be precise about what happened: the model didn't change. The product didn't change. The price didn't change. The switching cost — which everyone in this industry assumed was near-zero anyway — didn't change. What changed was who the operator is now, and what that implies about what the operator might do.

And hundreds of thousands of people uninstalled the app.

Values Alignment Just Showed Up as a Switching Trigger

The standard assumption about consumer AI tools has been: users are pragmatic, switching is frictionless, and people will go wherever the outputs are best. Identity doesn't enter into it. You're not what your chatbot is.

The DoD deal breaks that assumption in a specific and interesting way. This isn't users switching because Claude writes better code or because ChatGPT had a bad UX week. This is users switching because the operator's ethics became, for them, part of the product. The tool became identity-laden in the same way a consumer brand can become identity-laden — where what it stands for starts to matter alongside what it does.

As TechCrunch notes, OpenAI seems genuinely unequipped to manage its transition from consumer startup to national security infrastructure. That's a governance story. But the uninstall surge is a market story, and those are different animals.

The half-formed theory I'm sitting with: enterprise compliance teams have priced in values alignment for years — it shows up in vendor questionnaires, data processing agreements, ethics reviews. But the DoD data suggests that ordinary consumers are now doing a version of the same calculation, fast and viscerally, when the values gap is big enough. That's not a gradual trend. That's a threshold behavior.

Which raises the question I don't have an answer to: is this a one-off protest event, or is it the first visible instance of something structural? Protest behavior dissipates. Structural behavior compounds. The difference matters enormously for anyone building in this space.

What I'd watch for: whether the users who switched to Claude stay. If the uninstall surge is protest behavior, they drift back when the news cycle moves on. If it's structural — if they discover Claude is good enough and the values gap now functions as a retention driver — then the DoD deal will have done something stranger than anyone planned: it handed a competitor a cohort of users who arrived with a reason to stay loyal that has nothing to do with benchmark scores.

Nobody modeled that in their go-to-market.
